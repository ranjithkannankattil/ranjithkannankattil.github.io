Tax and money basics

  • You become Italian tax-resident once you live here most of the year (183+ days) or register residency. Italy then taxes your worldwide income; the India–Italy and Indonesia–Italy tax treaties prevent double taxation. Your Indian bank accounts may need to switch to NRO/NRE status.
  • Regime impatriati (for any nationality moving here): only 50% of qualifying work income is taxed for 5 years (60% exempt with a minor child), capped at €600,000 of income. You must not have been Italian tax-resident in the previous 3 years, must hold a qualified role, and must stay at least 4 years or pay it back. Ask HR or a commercialista before your first payslip.
  • Foreign assets (bank accounts, property, investments abroad) must be declared each year in the Quadro RW of your tax return, and IVIE/IVAFE wealth taxes may apply. People often miss this.
  • Employees usually file the simple 730 return through a CAF (free or cheap tax help centres) or the pre-filled online version.

Moving from the US

Americans can visit Italy visa-free for 90 days in any 180, but living here needs a long-stay (type D) national visa from the Italian consulate that covers your US state, then the permesso di soggiorno within 8 working days of arrival. Since October 2025 the EU Entry/Exit System records your fingerprints and photo at the border.

Paperwork from home: get birth and marriage certificates apostilled by the issuing state's Secretary of State (federal documents and FBI background checks by the US Department of State), then a sworn Italian translation. Do this before you leave; it takes weeks from abroad.

US taxes don't stop when you move. The US taxes by citizenship, so you keep filing every year on top of Italian taxes.

  • Form 1040 every year. Living abroad gives an automatic extension to 15 June (interest still runs from 15 April); you can extend further to October.
  • FBAR (FinCEN 114) if your non-US accounts together top $10,000 at any moment of the year. Your Italian current account, savings and pension pots all count. Penalties for missing it are severe.
  • FATCA Form 8938 for larger foreign assets. Thresholds for people living abroad are higher (for a single filer, $200,000 at year end or $300,000 at any time).
  • Avoid double tax with the Foreign Tax Credit (Form 1116) or the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (Form 2555, about $130,000 and indexed yearly). With Italian tax rates, the credit often works better. The US–Italy treaty doesn't remove the duty to file.
  • Don't buy Italian or EU funds and ETFs. The IRS treats them as PFICs with punitive tax and paperwork. Keep investing through a US broker that accepts a foreign address (some close accounts when you move abroad, so check before you go).
  • State taxes: some states (for example California, Virginia, New Mexico and South Carolina) can keep treating you as a resident. Break your domicile cleanly: driver's licence, voter registration, home.
  • On the Italian side, report US accounts and brokerage in the Quadro RW and expect IVAFE wealth tax; 401(k), IRA and Roth withdrawals are treated differently in Italy than in the US. The regime impatriati (above) is open to Americans too. Use an adviser who knows both systems.

Social Security and health

  • The US–Italy totalization agreement means you pay into only one social security system at a time; seconded employees ask for a certificate of coverage. Work credits in both countries can be combined toward a pension.
  • US Social Security can be paid straight into an account in Italy.
  • Medicare does not cover care abroad. Think through Part B enrolment before you move; late enrolment brings lifelong penalties if you return. US domestic insurance usually doesn't work here; employees get the free SSN, others need private international cover or SSN voluntary enrolment.

Day-to-day admin

  • Driving: no US state licence can be converted. You can drive with your US licence plus an International Driving Permit (from AAA) during your first year of residency, then you need the full Italian exams.
  • Banking: Italian banks ask Americans for a W-9 (FATCA) and some refuse US persons. Keep a US account and a US credit card with no foreign transaction fees; use Wise for transfers.
  • Keep a US phone number (port it to a cheap or VoIP plan) so bank and IRS two-factor codes keep reaching you, and use a US mail-forwarding service for statements.
  • Voting: register each year with the Federal Post Card Application at FVAP.gov. Enrol in STEP to get consulate alerts.
  • Passports: renew well ahead through the consulate; a US passport issued abroad still works for travel back.
  • Moving your things: personal goods can come in duty-free if they arrive within 12 months of taking up residence (customs want your residency papers). Italy runs on 230V/50Hz with type L and F plugs; most US appliances need transformers, so sell rather than ship. Importing a US car is rarely worth it.
  • Pets: ISO microchip first, rabies shot at least 21 days before travel, then a USDA-endorsed EU health certificate within 10 days of arrival.
  • Culture shock Americans mention most: smaller flats, no clothes dryer, little air conditioning, shops closed on Sundays, slower service, cash still used in small places, and paperwork that needs paper copies and stamps. Order a Thanksgiving turkey from a butcher weeks ahead.

Before you sign or pay

  • Deposit (deposito cauzionale) is capped by law at 3 months' rent; any clause above that is void. It earns legal interest and should come back within about 30 days of handing back keys, minus documented damage.
  • The landlord must register the contract with the Agenzia delle Entrate within 30 days. Ask for the registration receipt. An unregistered contract blocks your residency and permit renewal.
  • Check who owns the flat (a visura catastale costs a few euros) and that the person signing has the right to rent it.
  • Never pay a deposit for a flat you or someone you trust has not seen. Never accept keys "sent by post". These are the classic Milan scams on Facebook groups and Subito.
  • Agency fee (provvigione) is commonly 10–15% of one year's rent plus VAT, paid once. Ask before viewing.
  • Sign a photographed inventory and condition report on move-in day. Without it, damage is presumed yours.
  • Check the contract allows you to take residenza there. Some landlords refuse; you need it for almost everything.
  • After moving in: switch electricity and gas into your name (Enel, A2A, Edison, Iren and others), set up fibre broadband, and register for TARI (waste tax, paid by the occupant) with the Comune. The TV licence fee is billed inside the electricity bill.
  • Learn the AMSA waste sorting rules: paper, plastic+metal, glass, organic and general waste go out on set days. Fines are real.
Search sites: Immobiliare.it, Idealista, Casa.it, Subito (careful), Spotahome and HousingAnywhere for furnished mid-term, plus expat Facebook groups.

The one-year clock starts at your residenza date. Driving on a non-convertible foreign licence after that brings a fine of roughly €400–1,600 and confiscation. The theory test is only in Italian (since 2011). A driving school (autoscuola) package typically costs €800–2,000. Many expats start the theory course in month 6.

Owning a car: compulsory third-party insurance (RC auto), annual road tax (bollo), roadworthiness test (revisione) every 2 years after the first 4.

Driving in Milan: Area B and Area C

  • Area B covers almost the whole city. Mon–Fri 07:30–19:30 older polluting vehicles are banned (no ticket; you either qualify or you don't). Wrong entry: €83 fine plus fees.
  • Area C is the historic centre inside the Bastioni ring: a €7.50 daily ticket for most cars, linked to your number plate. Some 2026 sources report the charge being extended to weekends; check the Comune page before you drive in.
  • Check your plate on the Comune website before buying any used car. Diesel and older petrol cars face rolling bans.
  • Parking: blue lines are paid (EasyPark and similar apps), yellow lines are reserved for residents or permit holders, white lines are free.

Health & schools

Public care is good for emergencies and serious illness; waits for specialists can be long, so many expats combine the SSN with private insurance.

Pharmacies (green cross) give advice and many medicines; a rota pharmacy is always open at night. ER triage uses colour codes, so minor cases wait long.

Public health service (SSN) and the health card

  • Employees and their dependent family with a permit for work or family reasons have compulsory, free SSN enrolment. You get the tessera sanitaria and a family doctor; children get a paediatrician.
  • People who can't enrol compulsorily can use voluntary enrolment: since the 2024 budget law the minimum is €2,000 per year per calendar year (reduced for students and au pairs).
  • Visits to specialists need a GP referral (impegnativa) and a small fee (ticket); book through the regional booking line or the Lombardy health app. Records live in the Fascicolo Sanitario Elettronico.

Cigna and direct billing

  • Cigna has no public, fixed list of "verified" Milan hospitals; the network changes. Check each provider in your own Cigna portal before booking: Cigna Envoy (employer plans) or the Cigna Global app (individual plans). Providers that bill Cigna directly carry a "direct settlement may be available" note.
  • For planned care, ask Cigna for a guarantee of payment in advance; without it, private hospitals usually ask you to pay first and claim back.
  • Large private groups that commonly hold international insurer agreements: Gruppo San Donato (San Raffaele, Galeazzi), Humanitas, MultiMedica. Confirm with the hospital's international patients office and Cigna both.
  • Keep every invoice (fattura) and report; claims and Italian tax deductions both need them.

Other options (not 24/7)

  • McFIT (Città Studi, Certosa, Viale Fulvio Testi): budget chain, open 07:00–23:00 weekdays and 09:00–21:00 weekends. Flexible monthly plan about €49.90, cheaper on a 12-month contract.
  • Virgin Active: premium clubs with pools and classes across the city; long hours, but not overnight.
  • Palestre Italiane, Fit Express and many independent gyms: mid-price, neighbourhood-based.
  • Milanosport: the city's public sports centres and pools, cheapest option, with per-entry tickets.
  • FitPrime: one subscription for entries at many Milan gyms and studios, good if you move around the city.
  • Italian gyms ask for a medical certificate for non-competitive sport (certificato medico sportivo non agonistico) from your GP or a sports clinic. Bring it when you join.

Customs and culture

  • Greetings matter. Shake hands with new people; friends kiss on both cheeks. Use Lei (formal "you") with officials, doctors and older people.
  • Coffee at the bar: standing at the counter is cheaper than sitting. Cappuccino is a morning drink. "Un caffè" means an espresso.
  • Restaurants: a coperto (cover charge, €2–4) is normal. Tipping isn't expected; rounding up is kind. Dinner starts around 20:00.
  • Opening hours: many small shops close Sunday and Monday morning, and some at lunch. Supermarkets in Milan often open daily.
  • August: around Ferragosto (15 Aug) the city empties and many businesses, doctors and offices close for 2–3 weeks. Plan admin before.
  • 7 December, Sant'Ambrogio, is Milan's own public holiday (La Scala opens its season).
  • Bureaucracy: bring originals plus copies of everything, a marca da bollo (revenue stamp, from tobacconists) when asked, and patience. Being polite and persistent works better than escalating.
  • Condominium life: respect quiet hours (typically early afternoon and night, set by building rules), sort waste correctly, and greet the portinaio (building concierge) if you have one; they solve many problems.
  • Dress: Milan dresses smartly, even casually. Shorts are rare in offices; churches need covered shoulders and knees.
  • Cash and cards: shops must accept cards for any amount; small bars may still prefer cash. Keep some coins.
  • Always validate your transport ticket or tap in. Inspectors fine on the spot, tourists included.

Things people often miss

  • Bring documents with an apostille and sworn Italian translation from home: marriage certificate, children's birth certificates, degree certificates (for professional recognition). Getting them later from India or Indonesia is slow.
  • Family reunification (ricongiungimento familiare) needs proof of income and suitable housing size (an idoneità alloggiativa certificate).
  • Spouse's right to work depends on their permit type; a family permit usually allows work.
  • Heating: central heating in Milan runs from mid-October to mid-April by municipal rule. Winters are damp and grey; summers hot and humid. Air conditioning is not standard in rentals.
  • Pets: dogs must be microchipped and registered with the regional pet registry.
  • Home insurance is cheap and worth having for liability to neighbours (leaks are common in old buildings).
  • Sending money home: compare Wise and banks; keep records for the Quadro RW declaration.
  • Voting and Indian documents: keep Aadhaar/PAN mobile numbers active (roaming or eSIM) to receive OTPs; losing them is a common expat headache.
  • Mental load: the first winter is often the hardest. Joining one regular activity (a class, a sports club) helps more than anything.
